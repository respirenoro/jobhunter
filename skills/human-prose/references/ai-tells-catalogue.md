# Catalogue of AI writing tells

Source: Wikipedia, "Signs of AI writing" (WikiProject AI Cleanup field guide, current to October 2026) and its markup subpage. Read in full, including every collapsed example. The page is descriptive, not prescriptive: these are observations of how chatbot prose differs from human prose, not style rules. Re-read the live page (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing) when the user asks for an update, because the list shifts with each model generation.

## Why the tells exist

Language models regress to the mean. They pick what is statistically likely across the widest set of cases, then get tuned toward broadly pleasant prose. Specific, unusual, nuanced facts are rare, so they get replaced with generic, positive, important-sounding statements. A "revolutionary titan of industry" is what is left when "inventor of the first train-coupling device" fades. Every tell below is a version of that: less specific, more exaggerated, applicable to many subjects.

The page also warns: do not merely treat the signs as the problem. The real problems are vague content, claims nobody checked, and invented specifics. Fixing surface signs while leaving those makes the text harder to detect and no better.

## 1. Content tells

### Undue emphasis on significance, legacy, and broader trends
Puffs up the importance of an ordinary subject by tying it to a bigger theme.
Words and phrases: stands/serves as, is a testament/reminder, a crucial/pivotal/vital/significant/key role/moment, underscores/highlights its importance/significance, reflects broader, symbolizing its ongoing/enduring/lasting, contributing to the, setting the stage for, marking/shaping the, represents/marks a shift, key turning point, evolving landscape, focal point, indelible mark, deeply rooted, "distinction matters because", generated debate, prompted broader reflection, raising questions about, shaped emerging discussions, plays a role in the ecosystem, contributes to the broader history.
Also: hedging preambles that admit a subject is minor and then argue its importance anyway; over-connecting species or places to a wider ecosystem; belaboring conservation or research status that is unknown.

### Canned emphasis on notability, attribution, and media coverage
Claims importance by describing the coverage: independent coverage, local/regional/national media outlets, trade publications, featured/profiled/cited in, written by a leading expert, active social media presence, maintains a strong digital presence, was identified by, significant/substantial secondary coverage. Attributes the model's own analysis to a source ("Roger Ebert highlighted the lasting influence") whether or not the source says it. Echoes policy wording as if justifying itself.

### Superficial analysis (the -ing tail)
A present-participle phrase bolted to the end of a sentence to supply significance: "...creating a lively community", "...further enhancing its significance", "...reflecting its continued relevance", "...demonstrating the ongoing relevance of", "...highlighting their historical significance", "...underscoring the image the label aimed to cultivate".
Words: highlighting/underscoring/emphasizing, ensuring, reflecting/symbolizing, contributing to, cultivating/fostering, encompassing, enhancing, valuable insights, align/resonate with.
Newer tools may pin these to named sources whether or not the source says anything close.

### Promotional and advertisement-like language
Travel-guide and press-release tone even when told to be neutral. Words: boasts a, vibrant, rich, profound, enhancing, showcasing, exemplifies, commitment to, natural beauty, nestled, in the heart of, groundbreaking, renowned, featuring, diverse array, breathtaking, rich cultural heritage, gateway to, dependable, value-driven, "align with the company's goals", "emphasized commitment to", "communicates a powerful emotional presence", "showcasing the brand's dedication to craftsmanship". Cultural-heritage subjects get constant reminders of importance. Newer models are subtler and avoid blunt superlatives, but still lean positive.

### Vague expression of connection or association
Instead of stating a relationship ("was CEO of X", "taught science at Y"): in connection with, connected with/to, in association with, associated with, became associated with, has been associated with, associated principally with, "ventured into politics as a candidate" for "was a candidate", "began his career as" for "was". Often paired with puffery and AI vocabulary ("particularly/widely associated").

### Vague attributions and overgeneralized opinions
Weasel wording: industry reports, observers have cited, experts argue, some critics argue, described in scholarship, modern researchers treat, researchers and conservationists, according to [region] sources. Inflates the number of sources behind a view held by one or two. "Such as" before a list implies others exist when nothing says they do.

### Outline-like "challenges and future prospects" closers
"Despite its [positive words], [subject] faces challenges...", then a vaguely upbeat ending ("continues to thrive", "positions them as critical components", "adapt to these emerging trends"). Headings like "Challenges and Legacy", "Future Outlook", "Challenges and Future Directions". The sign is the rigid formula, not the mere mention of a challenge.

### "Awards and recognition" and "X and Y" headings
Near-ubiquitous in AI articles, far above promotional human writing.

## 2. Language and grammar tells

### High density of "AI vocabulary"
One or two is coincidence; many, repeatedly, is one of the strongest tells. They co-occur. The set changes over time. A word being overused does not mean its synonyms are, and context matters ("underscore" can be a literal underline or incidental music).

Era lists (rough, not hard cutoffs):
- 2023 to mid-2024 (GPT-4): Additionally, boasts, bolstered, crucial, delve, emphasizing, enduring, garner, intricate/intricacies, interplay, key, landscape, meticulously, pivotal, underscore, tapestry, testament, valuable, vibrant.
- Mid-2024 to mid-2025 (GPT-4o): align with, bolstered, crucial, emphasizing, enhance, enduring, fostering, highlighting, pivotal, showcasing, underscore, vibrant.
- Mid-2025 to mid-2026 (GPT-5): emphasizing, enhance, highlighting, showcasing, plus the canned-attribution words.
- Mid-2026 on (GPT-6): challenge, clearer, dependable, echoed, foster, leverage, matters, multifaceted, practical, prioritize, quietly, steady, universally. Output uses many more commas and periods and shorter sentences, around a strict 8 to 20 word band.
- Other words in the word-watch boxes: deep dive, robust, showcase, exemplifies, profound, rich, focal point, indelible, deeply rooted, resonate, cultivating, encompassing, valuable insights.
- Grok idiosyncrasy: superficially scientific words such as causal, empirical, correlate; still overuses underscore.
- "Additionally" opening a sentence is the classic. Transition words alone are a weak tell; "Consequently", "Notably", "Moreover" are overused only when formulaic.

### Avoidance of basic copulatives
"Serves as", "stands as", "marks", "functions as", "operates as", "represents a", "refers to", "boasts", "features", "maintains", "offers a" in place of plain "is", "are", "has". Studies found a drop of over 10 percent in "is/are" in academic writing in 2023. AI copyedits "improve" text this way: "is LAAA's exhibition arm" became "serves as LAAA's exhibition space" and "There are four spaces" became "features four separate spaces". Lead sentences use "refers to" as if defining a word.

### Negative parallelisms
- Not just X, but also Y / not only ... but ... / it is not just ..., it's ...
- Not X, but Y / it's not ..., it's ... / no ..., no ..., just ...
- Y rather than X (common in recent output): "prioritizing consolidation rather than purity", "a bridge ... rather than a replacement".
- Multi-sentence versions: "...which took a path that intertwined both ambition and complexity."
These can sound like correcting a misconception nobody raised.

### Rule of three
Adjective, adjective, adjective; short phrase, short phrase, and short phrase. Used to make shallow analysis look thorough, and stronger as a tell where nobody would bother (summaries, short replies).

### Elegant variation (older models)
Repetition penalties made older models rotate synonyms for the same thing ("the constraints of socialist realism", "the challenging climate of Soviet artistic constraints", "the confines of state-imposed norms"). Human writing repeats the plain word. Non-native writers may also avoid repetition, so this is a soft sign.

### Leads treating titles or lists as proper nouns
"X refers to...", "The list of Y is a curated compilation of..."

## 3. Style tells

- Title Case In Headings: capitalizes all main words.
- Overuse of boldface: bolding every instance of a key phrase, "key takeaways" style.
- Inline-header vertical lists: bullet or number, then a bold header, then a colon, then description ("**Route Details**: ..."). Bullets may be pasted as •, -, –, #, or emoji.
- Emoji as formatting: emoji before headings and bullets, "Let's Connect!" sign-offs.
- Unusual tables: small, minimally formatted tables that should be prose.
- Heading errors: a title heading before content, skipped heading levels, level-1 overuse, headings that only hold other headings.
- Curly quotation marks and apostrophes: ChatGPT and DeepSeek typically use them, sometimes mixing with straight ones; Gemini and Claude typically do not. Curly quotes alone prove nothing (Word, macOS and iOS smart quotes).
- Em dashes: LLMs use them where humans use commas, parentheses, colons or hyphens, in a punched-up sales cadence, often spaced. A July 2026 study found Claude the only contemporary model using more em dashes than professional writers. Newer models are hard-coded to avoid them, which collapses punctuation variety to commas and periods, and that uniform plainness is now its own sign.
- Section summaries: "In summary", "In conclusion", "Overall", closing sections that restate the point, paragraphs ending with a restatement.

## 4. Communication-to-the-user leaks

- Collaborative phrases: Certainly!, Of course!, I hope this helps, You're absolutely right!, Would you like..., Is there anything else, Let me know, here is a..., a more detailed breakdown, "check/verify/confirm ... before publishing/submitting".
- Pasted correspondence or prewriting meant for the user, including advice about how to use the text.
- Knowledge-cutoff statements (older): "As of my last knowledge update".
- Prompt refusals (older): "As an AI language model", "I'm sorry".
- Phrasal templates and placeholders left unfilled: [Your Name], [Article Title 1], (Add your channel URL here), INSERT_SOURCE_URL, 2025-XX-XX, example.com links.
- Abrupt cut-offs mid-sentence.
- Reasoning leaking into output (the "collapsed context" effect), including "Claude responded:" style preambles.

## 5. Hedging disclaimers about sources and recommended usage

- 2023-24 didactic: "it's important to note", "it is crucial to differentiate X from Y to prevent confusion", "it's important to remember".
- 2024-25 retrieval-era: "While specific details are limited/scarce", "not widely documented/available/disclosed", "in the provided/available sources/search results", "based on available information", "maintains a low profile", "keeps personal details private", speculation about what the unknown information "likely" is.
- Mid-2026 on, the most common current form: corrective framings. "X should be treated as Y rather than Z"; "does not by itself establish"; "does not establish that"; "should not be confused with". Often negative parallelism with "rather than".

## 6. Edit-summary and procedural-statement tells

Canned assurance of compliance: ensured that ... adheres to, refined, enhanced, enriched, streamlined, improved, in compliance with, neutral tone, encyclopedic tone, verifiability, clarity, flow, "improved sourcing and neutrality".
Procedural claims about what was kept or avoided: preserved/preserving, retained/retaining, avoided/avoiding, ensured/ensuring, aimed to, "while preserving the original meaning".
Overemphasis on sources: added sourced content, with reliable/independent/secondary sources, improved attribution.
Overemphasis on markup names and on addressing reviewer feedback. A human describes what changed, briefly; an AI describes how well the change complies.
Rigid formulas: the more a summary deviates, the less likely it is AI.

## 7. Markup and artifact tells (from the markup subpage)

- Markdown in the wrong place: **asterisks** for bold, ## for headings, [text](url) links, --- or ---- section dividers, triple-backtick code fences, backtick-wrapped terms.
- Leaked tool artifacts: contentReference[oaicite:...], oai_citation, turn0search0, attached_file, ppl-ai-file-upload, :::writing, "+1" citation counters, ↩ footnote returns.
- utm_source=chatgpt.com, utm_source=openai, utm_source=copilot.com, referrer=grok.com in URLs.
- Fabricated or unverifiable references: broken links, DOIs to unrelated papers, ISBNs with bad checksums, book citations without page numbers, citations that do not support the claim.

## 8. Signs of human writing (what to do more of)

Empirically more common in human text over 25 years of Wikipedia:
- Simple is/has phrases: there is a, it has a.
- Plain words over stiff synonyms: wrote (not authored), moved (not relocated), used (not utilized), tried (not attempted), died (not passed away).
- Superlative or definitive statements when true: one of the best, is the only, was the first.
- Qualifiers and intensifiers: very, perhaps, tends to.
- Isolated wordy constructions: as a result of, in order to, all of the, a part of, the fact that.
- The ability to explain your own choices, specific detail, and writing style that stays consistent over time.

## 9. Indicators that do NOT prove AI (do not over-correct)

Perfect grammar; mixed casual and formal register; "bland" or "robotic" prose; fancy or academic prose in general; transition words in isolation; unsourced content; correct markup. Humans are bad at telling the difference (about random chance in a 2025 study), and detector tools have real error rates. Do not aim to "beat a detector". Aim to write specific, true, plain prose.
